Practical guide

How to remember English words for the long term

Trying to memorise a hundred translations in one evening is rarely sustainable. Choose useful phrases, retrieve them from memory and let the gaps between successful reviews grow.

9-minute read6 stepsUpdated 19 August 2026
On this pageShort answerSix stepsWeekly routineCommon mistakesHow the app helps

The short version

To keep a word available in memory:

learn it inside a clear phrase, try to recall it before seeing the answer, return to it at increasing intervals and use it in your own speech or writing.

Six practical steps

1. Choose something you will use

Vocabulary connected to your work, travel, conversations or favourite content has an obvious purpose. Start with phrases that are likely to appear in your real life, not a random dictionary page.

2. Save a unit of meaning

Do not reduce check out to one translation. Check out of a hotel and check out this video are different uses. A short context makes the intended meaning unambiguous.

3. Recall before revealing the answer

Read the prompt and give yourself a few seconds. Rereading builds familiarity; retrieval practice builds the ability to produce the answer when it is absent.

4. Rate the result honestly

A forgotten answer is “Again”. A correct answer that took real effort is “Hard”. The rating is not a grade — it is information used to schedule the next review.

5. Return at useful intervals

Do not review every card every day. Stable cards should appear less often and forgotten ones sooner. This is the job of FSRS.

6. Use the phrase outside the card

Write a message, make your own sentence or say the phrase aloud. Cards keep an expression accessible; actual use links it to a real communicative task.

A simple weekly routine

WhenWhat to doUseful limit
Every dayComplete the reviews that are due firstA short session without racing
2–3 times a weekAdd a small number of new phrasesOnly what the future queue can absorb
Once a weekRemove low-value cards and clarify contextQuality before quantity
In real lifeUse two or three phrasesA message, note or conversation

What usually gets in the way

  • Too many new words. A few days later they return as an unmanageable review queue.
  • One translation without context. Polysemous words create a false sense of mastery.
  • Revealing the answer immediately. This trains recognition rather than retrieval.
  • Choosing “Easy” just to dismiss a card. The scheduler receives a misleading signal.
  • Practising only EN → RU. Understanding a phrase does not guarantee that you can produce it.
